## Read the room as space, not only objects

- **Enclosure and openings** (pp. 16–33): examine floor, wall and ceiling together in plan and section. A furniture cluster, partial partition, change in ceiling or light can distinguish a zone without a full enclosure. Test sightlines, privacy, daylight and circulation; an implied visual boundary provides no automatic acoustic or fire separation.
- **Figure and ground; mass and void** (pp. 18–19, 86, 105–106): draw both the objects and the spaces left between them. A cabinet arrangement that looks ordered in elevation can leave fragmented or unusable circulation. Check whether the remaining space is a coherent activity area, route, or accidental leftover.
- **Geometry and height** (pp. 20–27): room proportion suggests opportunities, not a compulsory layout. Long rooms may support a sequence of related activities; curved boundaries need deliberate coordination with freestanding or built-in furniture. Compare seated and standing views and the section before deciding that a ceiling feels too high or low. Visual scale does not establish compliant clear height.
- **Transitions** (pp. 28–33): assess the approach, threshold, view beyond and arrival point together. Door location and swing affect usable wall length and movement; stairs connect activities and change the sequence of views. Route actual stair, access and egress dimensions to statutory review.

## Turn a concept into alternatives

The analysis → synthesis → evaluation → implementation cycle (pp. 39–43) complements the existing brief and approval workflow. Record the design criteria before selecting an attractive image. Compare arrangements against function, user experience, physical constraints and maintainability, then revise the proposal against the brief.

Distinguish a **tight fit**, closely shaped around a stable activity, from a **loose fit**, allowing movable furniture and changing activities (pp. 66–69). Fixed joinery can use an irregular perimeter effectively but can limit future change. Show at least the intended current use and a plausible changed use when flexibility is part of the brief; check access, power and storage in both. Do not equate flexibility with empty unprogrammed space.

In the actual concept comparison, include a brief plan-and-section check: occupied furniture and remaining routes in plan; enclosing heights, storage mass and seated/standing sightlines in section. If heights/openings are unknown, describe the conditional test and required survey rather than omit the section or invent dimensions. An elevation alone is not a section. State the focal hierarchy and the criteria for choosing an option. When future flexibility is requested, explicitly compare a fixed/built-in arrangement with a movable/adaptable arrangement and test the changed use in both; two movable layouts alone do not expose the fixed-versus-flexible tradeoff.

## Vocabulary for a useful critique

| Principle | Design action | Check before adopting |
|---|---|---|
| Point, line, plane, volume (pp. 87–106) | Identify the focal object, directional edges, enclosing planes and occupied/empty volumes | Does the hierarchy support the activity and route? |
| Texture and pattern (pp. 99–104) | Compare tactile texture with visual texture; examine pattern size and repetition | View samples at intended distance and under intended light; small-scale pattern may merge visually |
| Proportion and scale (pp. 123–130) | Compare parts with each other, the room and the people using it | A preferred geometric ratio is a compositional tool, not proof of comfort or a universal beauty formula |
| Balance (pp. 131–136) | Choose axial symmetry, a radial grouping, or asymmetric visual balance | Account for apparent weight from size, contrast, texture and location, not merely equal object counts |
| Harmony and variety (pp. 137–139) | Give varied pieces a shared material, line, colour or shape; allow deliberate difference | Avoid a room where every element competes or where repetition erases useful distinction |
| Rhythm (pp. 140–143) | Repeat or grade elements and intervals along a wall or movement sequence | Coordinate visual rhythm with actual openings, services and use; do not force equal spacings across functional constraints |
| Emphasis (pp. 144–146) | Set a primary focus and subordinate supporting elements | Test from arrival and occupied viewpoints; not every wall needs a competing feature |

## Colour, light and material together

Use **hue** for colour family, **value** for lightness/darkness and **saturation** for intensity (pp. 107–121). Describe all three when revising a palette: “less saturated at similar value” is more actionable than “softer.” Review the distribution of light/dark areas and dominant/supporting/accent areas, not only isolated swatches. No fixed percentage formula is required.

Apparent colour depends on the light and neighbouring surfaces (pp. 113–117). Warm/cool, advancing/receding and emotional descriptions are conditional tendencies; they are not guaranteed outcomes or universal cultural responses. Compare physical samples together under project daylight and proposed electric light before approval. A screen image or matching colour name across paint, textile and laminate is not evidence of an identical finished appearance.

Treat lighting as part of the composition as well as a visibility requirement (chapter 6, pp. 249–257, 275–279). Consider background, task and accent relationships, reflected glare and texture modelling. Lux alone does not describe visual comfort; a dramatic accent cannot replace adequate task illumination. ## Concept-review output

Provide an annotated plan **and section/elevation**, a short comparison of alternatives, and a material/colour sample proposal. For each choice state: intended experience → spatial/compositional move → functional constraint → evidence or mock-up needed → client decision. Record open technical checks separately before detailed design. This extends the concept/SD artifacts in [deliverables](templates/deliverables.md).
