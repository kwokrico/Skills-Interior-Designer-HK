# Design research, briefing and communication (HKIDA / HKRIDA collection)

Load for design briefs, concept development, precedent/history research, presentations, drawing communication, education or competency planning. This is a synthesis of educational guidance, not a mandated design style or statutory qualification system. Source filenames, editions and PDF page locators are in [HKRIDA-INDEX.md](../../Reference/HKRIDA-INDEX.md).

## Brief and concept development

Sources: Design volume (`d7j83`), chapters 1–5, especially PDF pp. 33–46; Communication volume (`fgwSm`), PDF pp. 4–18; Human Environment volume (`Sc4da`), chapter 1.

1. Separate the commissioning client from actual users, operators and maintainers. Record activities, user diversity, cultural practices, privacy, storage, operational workflows, budget, programme and approval responsibilities. Use interviews, observation and measured site evidence; keep preferences distinct from observed needs.
2. Translate needs into an approved brief: objectives, room/activity schedule, areas, adjacency and separation requirements, circulation, existing constraints, success measures and unresolved questions. Distinguish measured facts, client instructions and proposals. Obtain approval before developing the preferred scheme.
3. Research the site at room, building and neighbourhood scales: access, daylight, views, noise, services, existing fabric, local culture and connections to surrounding public space. Assess opportunities for retaining and adapting what exists.
4. Use precedent studies to explain spatial organisation, proportion, construction, materials and social context. The Design volume compares Western, Chinese/Lingnan and Hong Kong traditions, modernism and postmodernism; avoid reducing these to an interchangeable decorative motif list. Verify specific dates, authorship or heritage status before relying on them.
5. Derive a concise concept from the brief and research. Test its consequences in plan, section, volume, light, material and movement. Explore alternatives through sketches, adjacency diagrams, physical/digital models and sample assemblies; compare functional fit, accessibility, buildability, cost and environmental effects.
6. Explain why the selected scheme meets the brief and why alternatives were rejected. Preserve the decision trail across SD, DD and technical detailing. A concept must remain legible in the details; visual appeal alone does not demonstrate functional or technical success.

## Choose communication for the decision

Source: Communication volume, PDF pp. 7–30.

| Stage / decision | Useful representation | Record before proceeding |
|---|---|---|
| Inception and feasibility | Written brief, existing-condition survey, activity/adjacency diagrams | Agreed scope, constraints, budget and decision makers |
| Concept / SD | Sketches, bubble diagrams, concept model, mood images, proportioned plans, perspectives or walkthrough | Preferred option, client comments, revision and approval |
| DD | Coordinated plans, sections/elevations, material boards, FF&E and lighting schedules, selected mockups | Dimensions, finishes, interface decisions and outstanding selections |
| Tender / CD | Revision-matched drawings, details, schedules and specification; drawing/document register | Issue purpose, recipients, date/revision, unresolved discrepancies |
| Construction | Site photographs, meeting minutes, marked-up sketches, RFI/SI/VO records | Location, author, attendees, decision, action owner and due date |
| Completion / use | Snag photographs, record drawings, O&M, user survey/interview and site review | Defect closure separately from performance and user feedback |

Renderings and VR communicate spatial intent; samples establish actual colour, texture and light response; prototypes test mechanisms and assemblies. Use the representation that resolves the uncertainty. Do not use a rendering as evidence of dimensions, compliance or product performance. Show materials under intended light and review transitions together.

Use the master skill's scale defaults. The book's sample paper sizes, example scales and presentation dress etiquette are contextual examples, not mandatory standards. A construction set needs a drawing index, title blocks, dates, revisions, legends, coordinated tags and distribution record. Clearly identify design intent versus fabrication/shop drawings and the party responsible for each.

Site sketches and verbal decisions that affect scope, cost, time or construction must enter the formal instruction/change workflow within the appointment's authority. Meeting minutes preserve decisions but do not automatically amend the contract. Project descriptions should explain the problem, concept, constraints and decision rationale, credit collaborators and obtain permission before publishing client information or images.

## Education and competence

Sources: 2014 Professional Guideline, PDF pp. 10–18; Design volume, chapters 3–5.

The six knowledge domains are human environment needs; design; products/materials; communication; interior construction/codes/regulations; professional practice. Review a portfolio or learning plan against all six, rather than judging drawing ability alone. Combine studio projects, observation, technical exercises, prototypes, critique, reflective design records and supervised practice.

The guideline proposes education, professional experience/logbooks and continuing education. A useful logbook records project stage, tasks, role, mentor review and learning gaps; continuing education should address actual gaps in codes, materials, technology and practice. Its proposed Parts 1–3, qualification levels, durations and CPD/CEU tables are historical recommendations. They do not establish current HK registration eligibility, protected title rights or permission to perform AP/RSE work; verify the current scheme when asked about credentials.
