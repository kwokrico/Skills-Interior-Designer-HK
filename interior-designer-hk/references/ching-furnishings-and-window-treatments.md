# Furnishings, storage and window treatments

Use for furniture grouping, flexible layouts, storage briefs, window coverings and accessory coordination. Source: Ching and Binggeli, *Interior Design Illustrated*, third edition (2012), chapter 8, printed pp. 317–352 (PDF pp. 325–360); layout strategy also pp. 66–69. [Archived source](<../../Reference/SS/Francis D.K. Ching & Corky Binggeli - INTERIOR DESING ILLUSTRATED (3rd Edition).pdf>). Guidance below is a design checklist, not a product specification. Current product evidence and project requirements control dimensions, fire performance and installation.

## Furniture is a space-planning system

Group furnishings around activities before selecting matching styles (pp. 319–321). Compare individual movable pieces, built-in arrangements and modular systems for continuity, future rearrangement and usable space. Draw occupied positions and operating envelopes as well as furniture footprints: pulled-out chair, open drawer, extended bed and the route remaining around them. Use actual product drawings and the anthropometrics module; the book's example sizes are not universal minima.

Assess seating and upholstery together (pp. 324–329): user support, duration of use, construction, cleaning and wear. A decorative fabric or appealing silhouette alone does not establish suitability. For workstations (pp. 334–335), coordinate furniture, equipment, power/data and storage with the user's tasks. Route service provision to MEP; do not place an inaccessible outlet behind fixed casework.

## Storage starts with an inventory

For each category record the items, dimensions/quantity, frequency of access, desired visibility and foreseeable change (pp. 340–345). Select open shelves, doors, drawers or specialist fittings accordingly. Deep shelves may hide frequently used items; pull-outs change retrieval and clearance needs. Separate display from bulk storage. Confirm reach, opening paths, lighting, cleaning and equipment access before fixing elevations. Coordinate built-in supports and fixings through millwork/interface review; a textbook sketch is not an engineered support detail.

## Window-treatment brief and coordination

The book distinguishes shutters/blinds, fabric curtains/drapery and shades (pp. 346–349). Choose by required view, privacy, light filtering/darkening, appearance and operation rather than fabric alone. For each opening record:

- Day and night privacy; view retained; daylight/glare control; whether room darkening is required.
- Opening type, handle/sash movement, reveal depth and mounting location; space occupied when fabric or slats are stacked or raised.
- Control location and user reach; manual or motor operation; power and maintenance access if motorised.
- Material sample, cleaning method and replacement access; interaction with adjacent joinery, ventilation openings and lighting.

Check these in plan, elevation and section. A blackout fabric alone does not demonstrate a blackout room: assess light paths at edges and the head. Thermal, acoustic and fire claims require evidence for the proposed product/assembly; do not import generic claims about all curtains or blinds. Obtain current child-safety and installation requirements for the selected system before specification. Route code-sensitive openings and escape/access requirements to statutory/fire review.

## Accessories belong in the brief and budget

Coordinate functional accessories, artwork, collections and plants early (pp. 350–352). Record items retained or commissioned, placement, display/support needs, lighting, care and budget. Let personal collections inform hierarchy and grouping instead of treating accessories as a last-minute matching purchase. Confirm support and maintenance with the relevant specialist; artwork, plants or display plinths must not obstruct required routes or controls.
