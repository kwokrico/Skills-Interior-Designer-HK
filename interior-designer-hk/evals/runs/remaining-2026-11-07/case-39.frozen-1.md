# Living–dining room concept — provisional design intent

Make one wall the main focus, quieten the others, and organise the long room as related living, dining and future working zones. The golden ratio can help compose a panel or artwork grouping; it cannot guarantee harmony. Nor can I guarantee that blue will make the room feel larger: perceived space depends on lightness, saturation, lighting, contrast, furnishings and the viewer.

Before a dimensioned layout, I need the room length/width, ceiling and beam heights, door/window positions and swings, photos of the competing walls, retained furniture sizes, dining seat count, work equipment and any mobility needs. Final fit and construction decisions remain on hold pending those critical inputs under the skill’s compliance rule. The following is a concept to test, not a measured plan.

| Design move | Intended result | Test before approval |
|---|---|---|
| Select one primary focal wall, judged from arrival and the sofa | Clear hierarchy | Choose whether the view, artwork or TV should lead; avoid giving all three equal contrast and accent light |
| Bring the other feature walls into a common quiet background | Reduce competition | Trial similar lightness, restrained pattern and one recurring material; retain useful character in a small secondary element |
| Repeat a small number of lines, materials and accents across zones | Rhythm and continuity | Align important edges where practical, without forcing spacing across doors or usable furniture areas |
| Preserve coherent open space around furniture groups | Balance solid furniture with usable void | Draw people, occupied chairs and routes as well as furniture footprints |

Use the golden ratio approximately **1:1.618** only where it improves the composition—for example, as an initial comparison of a focal panel’s width and height. A roughly 62:38 split can be a sketch exercise for major and supporting areas, but must yield to dining seats, circulation, openings and the future desk. It is not a required room division or universal beauty formula.

Compare these two arrangements before committing to built-ins:

| | A — fitted arrangement | B — movable arrangement, preferred starting point |
|---|---|---|
| Current use | Built-in dining bench and reserved work bay integrated with storage; living seating grouped separately | Freestanding dining group and sofa group; a console occupies the future work position |
| Future work | Work bay receives desk equipment; fixed dimensions must already accommodate knees, chair movement, equipment, storage and accessible power | Replace or move console for an ergonomically suitable desk; move a loose chair or side table while retaining dining capacity |
| Strength / trade-off | Strong visual continuity and tailored storage, but expensive to change if future work needs grow | Easier to adapt and trial, but loose pieces need deliberate alignment and storage planning |
| Required test | Show both current and future occupied furniture envelopes; verify that fixed storage and bench do not trap the desk chair or obstruct the route | Test dining chairs pulled out and desk occupied simultaneously; ensure relocated furniture has a real destination |

The choice should follow measured fit, daily dining use, storage needs and how certain the future work brief is. A fold-away or console surface is not automatically adequate for sustained computer work.

Concept plan, not to scale; order to be reversed or adjusted after locating entry, kitchen and windows:

```text
[ dining group ] — [ living group / primary focus ] — [ future work pocket ]
[ continuous usable route beside the groups, with doors and chairs operating ]
```

Use the room’s length as a sequence of activities, with furniture orientation and restrained lighting distinguishing zones. Avoid making every zone a separate feature wall. Locate dining with regard to kitchen access; locate work with regard to usable daylight, screen glare, privacy and power. These positions are proposals, not known site conditions.

Section check through living and work zones: survey floor-to-ceiling height, beam underside, window sill/head and furniture heights. Trace the seated sofa view and seated desk view past storage to the openings, then check standing sightlines along the room. If storage interrupts daylight or the main view, reduce its height or relocate it. Keep the ceiling treatment visually continuous unless the surveyed volume supports a deliberate change; do not lower it just to achieve a ratio. A low visual divider would not provide acoustic privacy for calls.

For colour, test a **light-value, low-saturation blue** against a similarly light neutral, alongside the existing floor, sofa and proposed accent material. Keep hue, lightness and saturation distinct: dark saturated blue can carry considerable visual weight even though its hue is cool. Use large physical samples on the actual walls and inspect them from entry, sofa and desk positions in daytime and under the intended evening lights. Ask the household which version feels calmer, more open and comfortable; make no size guarantee from a screen rendering.

Use gentle ambient light to support continuity, task light for dining/work and restrained accent light for the main focus. Check reflected glare on the TV, screen and glossy surfaces. Avoid equally bright spotlights on each feature wall, which would recreate the competition.

The next design decision is the preferred focal wall and fitted-versus-movable approach. A measured 1:50 plan with occupied clearances, a section, a focal-wall elevation and an in-room sample review can then resolve the layout without sacrificing dining or the future work corner.
