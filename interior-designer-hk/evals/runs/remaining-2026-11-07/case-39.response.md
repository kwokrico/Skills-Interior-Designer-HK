# Living-room concept: one focal wall, three related activities

Create harmony by giving **one wall the leading role**, making the other feature walls quieter, and grouping furniture around living, dining and future work. The golden ratio can help organise an elevation; it cannot guarantee harmony. Blue may contribute to an airy impression, but **I cannot guarantee that it will make the room feel larger**.

This is a concept proposal for your HK home. A dimensioned layout needs the room measurements, doors and windows, existing furniture sizes, dining headcount and work requirements. Under the skill’s missing-critical-input rule, furniture sizing and a confirmed fit remain on hold; the following design direction can proceed without inventing those facts.

| Design move | Intended effect | Check before committing |
|---|---|---|
| Choose one primary wall from the entrance and main seated viewpoints | A clear focal point instead of competing features | Compare the existing walls, daylight, TV glare and any view worth retaining |
| Refinish secondary walls in a shared, low-contrast background; reduce their pattern and accent lighting | Let the focal wall stand out while the room reads as a whole | Retain useful storage; avoid replacing sound work solely for visual novelty |
| Repeat one timber tone, a related line or edge detail, and selected blue accents across the zones | Harmony and rhythm with enough variety to identify each activity | Check actual samples together and avoid repeating a feature at every available surface |
| Use furniture groups and lighting to mark zones | Break the long room into usable places without unnecessarily enclosing it | Draw the empty spaces as carefully as the furniture, including chair pull-out and door swings |

Use the golden ratio **selectively**: trial a roughly 62:38 division between a principal panel and a quieter supporting area on the chosen feature wall. This is an optional compositional study, not a prescribed room-area split. Align it with real openings, storage needs and viewing positions; abandon the ratio if it makes furniture or circulation awkward. Balance depends on contrast, texture and apparent visual weight as well as geometry. [Spatial composition reference](../../../references/ching-spatial-composition.md)

Compare two arrangements before fixing joinery:

| Option | Living and dining | Work corner now → later | Main test |
|---|---|---|---|
| A: distinct activity groups | Dining near the kitchen connection if the measured plan permits; living forms its own seating group | Movable reading chair and lamp → desk, suitable chair and equipment storage | Can dining chairs and an occupied desk coexist with the main route? |
| B: shared flexible table | Dining table also supports occasional laptop use; living retains a separate focal group | Reserve a defined perimeter bay for a later dedicated desk | Can work be packed away for meals, and does the future bay actually fit the equipment and user? |

Prefer A if it fits comfortably; B may suit intermittent work better. Avoid a decorative shallow console being treated as a guaranteed future workstation. Test knee space, chair movement, screen position, storage access, reachable power/data and cable routes in both the current and future layouts. Loose furniture keeps change possible; an unplanned leftover corner does not. [Furnishings reference](../../../references/ching-furnishings-and-window-treatments.md)

For the colour study, try a **light-value, low-saturation blue** alongside the retained flooring and proposed timber, with a related light neutral on the supporting walls. Hue alone does not determine spaciousness: darkness/lightness, saturation, daylight, neighbouring finishes and electric light all matter. Compare this with a neutral-wall option using blue only in textiles. Put large sample boards on both long and short walls and review them from the entrance and sofa in daylight and evening light before choosing.

Use gentle ambient light across the room, a controlled accent at the primary focus, dining light related to the table, and independently controlled task light at the work corner. Check screen reflections and seated glare; a dramatic feature light cannot substitute for useful work lighting.

For the next design decision, provide a measured sketch and photographs showing all walls, doors/windows and existing features, plus dining numbers, retained furniture, work equipment/use duration and budget. The next deliverable should be two 1:50 test-fits with occupied furniture envelopes, a focal-wall elevation and a physical sample comparison. Select the scheme by daily usability and your response to the samples, then refine its proportions.
