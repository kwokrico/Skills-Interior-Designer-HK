# Human environment, perception and universal use

Source: [Human Environment Needs volume](../../../../Reference/SS/HKRIDA/application_20200331_Sc4da.pdf), chapters 1–5, especially PDF pp. 22–40; Design volume chapter 5 (see [collection index](../../../../Reference/HKRIDA-INDEX.md)). Educational synthesis; numerical examples and dated rating credits are not current code.

## Understand users before dimensions

Distinguish the client, occupants, visitors, carers, operators and maintenance staff. Observe activities, movements, privacy/territorial needs, cultural practices and sensory sensitivities. Test a proposed layout against actual tasks and operating scenarios; an average adult dimension cannot establish suitability for every user. Use relevant population data and individual trials, including mobility aids and carer assistance where needed.

Apply the seven universal-design principles as review questions:

| Principle | Check |
|---|---|
| Equitable use | Can people share a dignified route and access the same functions? |
| Flexibility in use | Are handedness, posture, pace and different abilities accommodated? |
| Simple, intuitive use | Does the operation make sense without specialist knowledge? |
| Perceptible information | Are important cues available through appropriate visual, tactile or audible means? |
| Tolerance for error | Are foreseeable mistakes, collisions and pinch points prevented or mitigated? |
| Low physical effort | Can users operate controls and doors without awkward force or posture? |
| Size and space for approach/use | Are reach, transfer, manoeuvring, assistance and equipment accommodated? |

These principles guide inclusion; statutory accessibility is a separate check in the statutory module. Test routes, door operation, controls, sanitary transfer, seating and signage together. A wheelchair turning circle alone does not establish an accessible space.

## Coordinate environmental perception

Review colour/hue/chroma, texture, daylight, glare and contrast with the actual finish and lighting sample. Consider wayfinding and visual impairment; avoid assuming that a colour has the same emotional meaning for all cultures and users.

For thermal comfort, discuss air temperature, mean radiant temperature, air speed, humidity, clothing and activity together. PMV predicts mean thermal sensation; PPD estimates dissatisfaction for the stated conditions. Do not infer comfort from thermostat temperature alone or copy a historical comfort-model edition without checking its applicability with the MEP specialist.

For sound, distinguish sound level/background noise, room reverberation, absorption and partition isolation. STC/Rw, NRC and NC describe different things; do not substitute one for another. Chain to acoustics for quantified assessment and use complete assembly evidence rather than a bare-panel table as the installed rating.

For air quality, identify contaminant sources, outdoor intake conditions, moisture and ventilation strategy. Natural ventilation can conflict with outdoor noise, pollution, humidity and safety; an open window is not automatically a suitable solution. Coordinate source control, moisture management, fresh air and maintainable systems. Suspected hazardous existing materials need competent investigation before disturbance.

## Evaluate after occupation

Source: Communication volume PDF p. 5; Construction/Codes volume PDF pp. 56–58.

Agree success criteria at briefing, then review actual use with surveys, interviews, observation and relevant comfort/energy measurements. Record user groups, conditions, operational settings and limitations. Compare feedback with the approved brief, identify corrective actions and check results. Separate user satisfaction/performance improvements from contractual defects; neither a good photograph nor an empty snag list proves the design works for its occupants.
